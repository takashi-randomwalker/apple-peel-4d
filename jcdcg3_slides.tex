\begin{frame}{Face-rotation BFS}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.61\textwidth}
      \small
      Fix a \alert{root cell} $r$. Three steps.

      \begin{enumerate}
        \item[\textbf{1}] \textbf{Face-up.} Rotate in $SO(4)$ so the
          centroid of $r$ sits on the $+w$ axis.
        \item[\textbf{2}] \textbf{BFS.} Take a breadth-first spanning
          tree of the cell-adjacency graph from $r$. For each tree edge
          (parent $p$, child $c$), rotate $c$ about the shared polygon
          until it lands in $p$'s hyperplane.
        \item[\textbf{3}] \textbf{Project.} All cells now share one
          $w$-coordinate (spread $<4\times10^{-13}$). Drop $w$.
      \end{enumerate}
      \smallskip

      The result is a net iff no two cells share interior volume.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.37\textwidth}
      \centering
      \movie[loop]%
        {\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{talk_8cell_unfold_poster.png}}%
        {talk_8cell_unfold.mp4}\\
      {\scriptsize Opening one BFS layer at\\a time, into Dal\'{\i}'s cross}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{\textbf{1:20 \quad (cumulative 3:00)}\\
    The slowest slide. The rotation is forced: the shared polygon is
    the hinge. Step 3 is a check, not an assumption --- the cells really
    do land in one hyperplane. Do not dwell on step 2; the next slide is
    about what it leaves open.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Click the picture} and let it run while you read the three
    steps. It opens \emph{by layer}: the six neighbours first, then a
    pause, then the last cell. On that pause, say that BFS fixes the
    layers.
    \\[2pt]
    If asked why the cells look bent: it is a 4D view, and it fades out
    as the net flattens. The last frame is the real net.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 2 hides a choice}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.68\textwidth}
      \small
      The layers are forced: $L_d$ is the set of cells at distance $d$
      from $r$, and no rule can change that.
      \smallskip

      The \alert{parent} is not. A cell in $L_d$ may touch several
      cells of $L_{d-1}$, and BFS takes whichever one the
      implementation happens to reach first.
      \smallskip

      \begin{alertblock}{A geometric rule need not help}
        For the 5-, 8-, 16- and 24-cell the symmetries fixing $r$ and
        $v$ permute the candidates transitively, so \alert{every}
        equivariant score --- height along $w$, turning angle, the
        volume form --- ties on all of them.
      \end{alertblock}
      \smallskip

      Ours breaks the tie by cell index, which is not equivariant
      either. \alert{We come back to this.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.29\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.62,

\begin{frame}{The validity test, and a trap}
  A net is valid if for every pair of cells not joined by a tree edge,
  \[
    \mathrm{Vol}_3\bigl(\mathrm{conv}(c_i)\cap\mathrm{conv}(c_j)\bigr)
    \;\le\; 10^{-8}.
  \]
  \medskip

  \begin{alertblock}{Boundary contact is not overlap}
    Testing with closed-region membership instead counts a shared face,
    edge or vertex as an intersection.
    That is common for pointed cells, and it wrongly reports
    \[
      \text{16-cell } 0/16, \quad
      \text{24-cell } 0/24, \quad
      \text{600-cell } 0/600 .
    \]
    Measuring \emph{volume} ignores contact of measure zero.
  \end{alertblock}
  \note{\textbf{1:20 \quad (cumulative 5:10)}\\
    Tell it as something that happened. The first run said these three
    polytopes failed completely.
    \\[2pt]
    But cells that meet along an edge do not overlap. The fix is one
    line of code. Without it the theorem comes out backwards.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Contrast: the apple-peel algorithm}
  A different family of spanning trees --- a greedy spiral/zonal
  ordering (Yoshino--Chaidee, \texttt{arXiv:2605.30373}).

  \centering
  {\small
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    & BFS & \multicolumn{2}{c}{apple-peel (RZ)}\\
    \cmidrule(lr){3-4}
    Polytope & valid & orderings & nets\\
    \midrule
    120-cell & all roots & $1440/1440$ & \alert{$0/1440$}\\
    600-cell & all roots & \alert{$0/2400$} & ---\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \raggedright\smallskip
  The 120-cell has an ordering for every start and \alert{never} a net;
  the 600-cell has no ordering at all.

  \begin{block}{Consequence}
    Having a valid \emph{ordering} and having a valid \emph{net} are
    independent properties.
  \end{block}
  \note{\textbf{1:30 \quad (cumulative 8:20)}\\
    The companion algorithm peels the polytope like an apple, one cell
    at a time.
    \\[2pt]
    Read the two rows as opposite failures. The 120-cell always finds
    an order, and the order is never a net. The 600-cell never finds an
    order at all.
    \\[2pt]
    Then the block: ordering and net are independent. Do not explain
    how the apple-peel rule works. Only the contrast matters.
    \\[2pt]
    The arXiv number is there for anyone who wants the preprint. No need
    to read it out --- just leave the slide up long enough to photograph.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{And more: the tie-breaking question}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \small
      The abstract left this open:
      \begin{quote}\small\raggedright
        ``Whether validity extends to all BFS tie-breakings \dots\
        remains open.''
      \end{quote}
      \smallskip

      BFS layers do not depend on the rule, so a BFS tree is just a
      choice of parent, one layer up, for each cell. They can then be
      counted with no rule at all.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \centering
      {\small\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2pt}
      \begin{tabular}{lrr}
        \toprule
        & trees / root & checked\\
        \midrule
        5-cell   & $1$          & all\\
        8-cell   & $6$          & all\\
        16-cell  & $20{,}736$   & all\\
        24-cell  & $32{,}768$   & all\\
        \midrule
        120-cell & ${\sim}1.8\times10^{44}$ & 20 random\\
        600-cell & ${\sim}1.8\times10^{65}$ & 20 random\\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \smallskip

  {\small\begin{sloppypar}
  \alert{$1{,}132{,}661$ trees, no self-intersection.}
  For the first four the rule is irrelevant. And BFS is not a vacuous
  restriction: the 600-cell (Devadoss--Harvey) and the 24-cell
  (Seamons 2026) are \alert{not all-net}.
  \end{sloppypar}}
  \note{\textbf{1:50 \quad (cumulative 10:10)}\\
    Quote the abstract first. This answers the question the abstract
    asked; it is not a side remark.
    \\[2pt]
    The idea: the layers depend only on distance from the root, so a
    BFS tree is one parent per cell from the layer above, and the family
    can be counted without naming a rule. Four exhausted, two sampled;
    if asked why only 20 per root, $10^{44}$.
    \\[2pt]
    The last sentence is the payoff: it answers ``why BFS and not every
    tree?''. Two of the six are not all-net, and BFS works on both. Say
    it as the reason, not an apology.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{If asked:} the 600-cell is their own Theorem 11; for the
    24-cell, Seamons \emph{confirms} their conjecture, in a 2026
    preprint I checked myself.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: ``BFS tree'' is bigger than what BFS returns}
  A traversal reaches a tree iff its parent choices admit a total
  order, i.e.\ iff the constraint digraph $p(v)\to q$ is acyclic.
  \medskip

  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    & BFS trees & reachable & \\
    \midrule
    8-cell  & $6$        & $6$      & $100\%$\\
    16-cell & $20{,}736$ & $4{,}896$  & $23.6\%$\\
    24-cell & $32{,}768$ & $2{,}592$  & $7.9\%$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \raggedright\medskip
  So of the $786{,}432$ trees verified for the 24-cell, only
  $62{,}208$ are reachable by any traversal.
  The obstruction is a \emph{cycle} in the constraints, not merely two
  cells sharing two candidate parents: the 16-cell has no such pair and
  still leaves three quarters of its trees unreachable.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: which rules were compared}
  \centering
  {\small
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    & queue & max-index & min-index\\
    \midrule
    Runcinated 24-cell      & $0/240$     & $0/240$     & $0/240$\\
    Runcitruncated 600-cell & $47/2640$   & $2635/2640$ & $2627/2640$\\
    Runcitruncated 120-cell & $2570/2640$ & $2640/2640$ & $2151/2640$\\
    \midrule
    Grand antiprism         & $320/320$   & $320/320$   & \alert{$0/320$}\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \raggedright\medskip
  {\small
  ``queue'' is the textbook BFS loop, neighbours scanned in index
  order. ``min/max-index'' choose, for each cell, the smallest or
  largest indexed candidate parent.
  \smallskip

  Uniformly random trees are harsher still: of the 61 uniform
  polytopes valid from every root under the queue rule, 13 fail within
  10 random trees, the grand antiprism among them --- and every one of
  the four polytopes with $2{,}640$ cells fails all ten, whether or not
  it is one of the 61.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- B3
\begin{frame}{Backup: the one that no rule could unfold}
  \small
  Runcinated 24-cell $t_{0,3}\{3,4,3\}$, 240 cells, $0/240$ under all
  three index rules. Ask instead for a \emph{symmetric tree}: a BFS
  tree invariant under $H\le\mathrm{Stab}(r)$ exists iff every cell has
  a candidate parent fixed by its stabilizer in $H$. Here
  $|\mathrm{Aut}| = 2{,}304$, with two cell orbits, and the largest
  such $H$ has order~$3$.
  \smallskip

  \centering
  {\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
    \toprule
    root orbit & $|\mathrm{Stab}(r)|$ & $\max|H|$
      & $H$-invariant & random\\
    \midrule
    $48$ (octahedra)          & $48$ & $3$ & $70/600$ & \alert{$0/600$}\\
    $192$ (triangular prisms) & $12$ & $3$ & $83/600$ & \alert{$0/600$}\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \raggedright\smallskip
  The control is the point: unconstrained trees at those roots give
  nothing. Transitivity on each orbit then gives a valid net from
  \alert{all 240 roots} --- not a polytope without a net, but one whose
  nets no index rule finds.
  \smallskip

  (No conflict with slide 4: there the candidates form one orbit under
  the \emph{full} stabilizer, so no invariant tree exists at all.)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: what does \emph{not} separate them}
  \small
  \textbf{Cell degree} fails both ways. The grand antiprism and the
  snub 24-cell have cells of degree~4 --- the poverty that stops
  apple-peel on the 600-cell --- yet are valid from every root, while
  the runcinated 24-cell has minimum degree~5 and is valid from none.
  Nor does high degree help: every prism has a cell of degree up
  to~$92$ and all 17 are fine, and the root-dependent runcitruncated
  120-cell carries cells of degree~$32$.
  \medskip

  \textbf{Angular defect at an edge} --- $2\pi$ less the dihedral
  angles meeting there, zero exactly when those cells tile $\RR^3$
  around it. For the regular six it is one number, and it orders them
  \emph{exactly} by how hard they proved to be: $148.4^\circ$,
  $90^\circ$, $77.9^\circ$, $31.6^\circ$, $10.3^\circ$, $7.4^\circ$.
  Among the uniform ones it breaks both ways: the omnitruncated
  120-cell shares its minimum defect, $0.736^\circ$, with the
  root-dependent runcitruncated 120-cell and is valid from every root,
  while the runcinated 24-cell sits at $10.5^\circ$, above many that
  are. Weighting by depth or by size does not repair it.
\end{frame}
