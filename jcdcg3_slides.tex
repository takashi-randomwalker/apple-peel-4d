\begin{frame}{Unfolding a 4-polytope}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
      A \alert{net} of a convex polytope: cut it open and lay the
      facets out flat, without overlap.
      \medskip

      In 3D this is D\"urer's problem, still open.
      \medskip

      In 4D the facets are \alert{3-dimensional cells} and the net
      lives in $\RR^3$.
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Turney (1984): all 261 nets of the 8-cell
        \item Devadoss--Harvey (2023): \emph{every} spanning tree works
          for the 5-, 8-, 16-cell
      \end{itemize}
      \medskip

      \alert{Left open: 24-cell, 120-cell, 600-cell.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.4\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{face_rotation_net_8cell.png}\\
      {\scriptsize A net of the 8-cell: Dal\'{\i}'s cross}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{\textbf{1:20 \quad (cumulative 1:40)}\\
    Cut open, lay flat, no overlap. In 3D this is D\"urer; in 4D the
    pieces are solids and the net is a 3D object you can print. Use the
    picture: the 8-cell is the tesseract, and the net our algorithm
    returns is exactly Dal\'{\i}'s cross --- one of Turney's 261. Worth
    saying that this is forced, not luck: cells of the 8-cell are
    adjacent unless opposite, so the BFS layers from any root are
    $1+6+1$, and every one of the 6 trees per root gives this same
    cross. Devadoss--Harvey settled the three small ones for
    \emph{every} spanning tree. Land on the last line: three polytopes
    left open --- that is where this talk starts.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 3
\begin{frame}{Face-rotation BFS}
  Fix a \alert{root cell} $r$. Three steps.
  \medskip

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{1}] \textbf{Face-up.} Rotate in $SO(4)$ so the
      centroid of $r$ sits on the $+w$ axis.
    \item[\textbf{2}] \textbf{BFS.} Take a breadth-first spanning tree
      of the cell-adjacency graph from $r$. For each tree edge
      (parent $p$, child $c$), rotate $c$ about the shared polygon
      until it lands in $p$'s hyperplane.
    \item[\textbf{3}] \textbf{Project.} All cells now share one
      $w$-coordinate (spread $<4\times10^{-13}$). Drop $w$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \medskip

  The result is a net iff no two cells share interior volume.
  \note{\textbf{1:20 \quad (cumulative 3:00)}\\
    Slowest slide of the talk --- the audience has to picture the
    rotation. Emphasise that the rotation is forced: the shared polygon
    is the hinge. Step 3 is a check, not an assumption --- the cells
    really do land in one hyperplane. Do \emph{not} dwell on step 2
    here; the next slide is about exactly what it leaves open.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 4
\begin{frame}{Step 2 hides a choice}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.57\textwidth}
      \small
      The layers are forced: $L_d$ is the set of cells at distance $d$
      from $r$, and no rule can change that.
      \smallskip

      The \alert{parent} is not. A cell in $L_d$ may touch several
      cells of $L_{d-1}$, and BFS takes whichever one the
      implementation happens to reach first.
      \smallskip

      \begin{alertblock}{A geometric rule need not help}
        For the 5-, 8-, 16- and 24-cell the symmetries fixing $r$ and
        $v$ permute the candidates transitively, so \alert{every}
        equivariant score --- height along $w$, turning angle, the
        volume form --- ties on all of them.
      \end{alertblock}
      \smallskip

      Ours breaks the tie by cell index, which is not equivariant
      either. \alert{We come back to this.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.39\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.95,
          cell/.style={circle,draw,inner sep=1.7pt,fill=white},
          every node/.style={font=\scriptsize}]
        \node at (-1.5,1.9) {$L_0$};
        \node at (-1.5,1.0) {$L_1$};
        \node at (-1.5,0.0) {$L_2$};
        \node[cell,label={[label distance=-2pt]above:$r$}] (r) at (0.6,1.9) {};
        \node[cell] (a) at (-0.6,1.0) {};
        \node[cell] (b) at (0.6,1.0) {};
        \node[cell] (c) at (1.8,1.0) {};
        \node[cell,label={[label distance=-2pt]below:$v$}] (v) at (1.2,0.0) {};
        \draw (r)--(a); \draw (r)--(b); \draw (r)--(c);
        \draw[dashed,thick,red!70!black] (b)--(v);
        \draw[dashed,thick,red!70!black] (c)--(v);
        \node[red!70!black,font=\normalsize] at (2.35,0.5) {?};
      \end{tikzpicture}

      \smallskip
      {\scriptsize Two admissible parents for $v$}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{\textbf{0:50 \quad (cumulative 3:50)}\\
    Short slide, but it is the one the referees will care about. Two
    beats only. One: BFS fixes the layers, not the parents. Two: a
    cleverer geometric rule does not repair it --- for those four the
    candidates are genuinely interchangeable under the symmetry group,
    so any equivariant score ties. Checked over every cell that has
    more than one candidate parent --- 1, 11 and 7 cells for the 8-,
    16- and 24-cell, all single orbits; the 5-cell has none at all.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{If pressed:} this is stated for those four because it
    \emph{fails} for the 120-cell --- there 50 of the 71 such cells have
    candidates in two or more orbits, so an equivariant rule could
    discriminate. We have not looked for one. Do not volunteer this;
    the paper claims nothing stronger.
    \\[2pt]
    Close with ``we come back to this'' and move on --- the answer is
    slide 8.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The validity test, and a trap}
  A net is valid if for every pair of cells not joined by a tree edge,
  \[
    \mathrm{Vol}_3\bigl(\mathrm{conv}(c_i)\cap\mathrm{conv}(c_j)\bigr)
    \;\le\; 10^{-8}.
  \]
  \medskip

  \begin{alertblock}{Boundary contact is not overlap}
    Testing with closed-region membership instead counts a shared face,
    edge or vertex as an intersection.
    That is common for pointed cells, and it wrongly reports
    \[
      \text{16-cell } 0/16, \quad
      \text{24-cell } 0/24, \quad
      \text{600-cell } 0/600 .
    \]
    Measuring \emph{volume} ignores contact of measure zero.
  \end{alertblock}
  \note{\textbf{1:20 \quad (cumulative 5:10)}\\
    Tell it as something that actually happened: the first run reported
    total failure for three polytopes. Cells that meet along an edge are
    not overlapping. The fix is one line of code, but without it the
    main theorem reads the other way round.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Contrast: the apple-peel algorithm}
  A different family of spanning trees --- a greedy spiral/zonal
  ordering (Yoshino--Chaidee).

  \centering
  {\small
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    & BFS & \multicolumn{2}{c}{apple-peel (RZ)}\\
    \cmidrule(lr){3-4}
    Polytope & valid & orderings & nets\\
    \midrule
    120-cell & all roots & $1440/1440$ & \alert{$0/1440$}\\
    600-cell & all roots & \alert{$0/2400$} & ---\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \raggedright\smallskip
  The 120-cell has an ordering for every start and \alert{never} a net;
  the 600-cell has no ordering at all.

  \begin{block}{Consequence}
    Having a valid \emph{ordering} and having a valid \emph{net} are
    independent properties.
  \end{block}
  \note{\textbf{1:30 \quad (cumulative 8:20)}\\
    The companion algorithm peels the polytope like an apple, one cell
    at a time. Read the two rows as a pair of opposite failures: the
    120-cell always finds an order and the order is never a net; the
    600-cell never finds an order at all. Conclusion in the block ---
    ordering and net are independent. Do not get drawn into how the
    apple-peel rule works; the point is only the contrast.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{And more: the tie-breaking question}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      The abstract left this open:
      \begin{quote}\small
        ``Whether validity extends to all BFS tie-breakings \dots\
        remains open.''
      \end{quote}
      \medskip

      BFS layers do not depend on the rule, so a BFS tree is just a
      choice of parent, one layer up, for each cell --- and the trees
      can be counted and enumerated with no rule at all.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \centering
      {\small
      \begin{tabular}{lrr}
        \toprule
        & trees / root & checked\\
        \midrule
        5-cell   & $1$          & all\\
        8-cell   & $6$          & all\\
        16-cell  & $20{,}736$   & all\\
        24-cell  & $32{,}768$   & all\\
        \midrule
        120-cell & ${\sim}1.8\times10^{44}$ & 20 random\\
        600-cell & ${\sim}1.8\times10^{65}$ & 20 random\\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \medskip

  \alert{$1{,}132{,}661$ trees, no self-intersection.}
  For the first four the rule is irrelevant --- including the 24-cell,
  one of the open cases.
  \note{\textbf{1:40 \quad (cumulative 10:00)}\\
    Quote the abstract first --- this answers the question the abstract
    asked, it is not a side remark. The idea: BFS layers are determined
    by distance from the root, so a BFS tree is nothing but one parent
    per cell chosen from the layer above. That makes the family
    countable without ever naming a rule. Four polytopes exhausted, two
    sampled. If asked why only 20 per root: $10^{44}$.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 9
\begin{frame}{$\triangle$ Warning: this does not extend}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \small
      Beyond the regular case the rule stops being harmless.
      \smallskip

      \begin{alertblock}{Grand antiprism, 320 cells}
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{ll}
          rule A & valid from \alert{all $320$} roots\\
          rule B & valid from \alert{no} root\\
        \end{tabular}
      \end{alertblock}
      \smallskip

      Same polytope, same roots, same family of BFS trees.
      Only the choice of parent differs.
      \smallskip

      Among the 64 convex uniform 4-polytopes this is not isolated:
      others swing from $1.8\%$ to $99.8\%$ of their roots.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{talk_gap.png}\\
      {\scriptsize Grand antiprism, one valid net}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \smallskip

  \centering
  \textbf{\small What regularity buys is not that some tree works,
  but that the choice does not matter.}
  \note{\textbf{1:40 \quad (cumulative 11:40)}\\
    The warning is the corollary of the previous slide, so say ``and
    this is exactly what fails once you leave the regular case''.
    Grand antiprism is the cleanest instance: all 320 or none,
    decided by nothing but which parent you pick. Then the closing
    sentence --- say it slowly, it is the line to remember.
    Backup B2 has the full rule comparison if it is questioned.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: ``BFS tree'' is bigger than what BFS returns}
  A traversal reaches a tree iff its parent choices admit a total
  order, i.e.\ iff the constraint digraph $p(v)\to q$ is acyclic.
  \medskip

  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    & BFS trees & reachable & \\
    \midrule
    8-cell  & $6$        & $6$      & $100\%$\\
    16-cell & $20{,}736$ & $4{,}896$  & $23.6\%$\\
    24-cell & $32{,}768$ & $2{,}592$  & $7.9\%$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \raggedright\medskip
  So of the $786{,}432$ trees verified for the 24-cell, only
  $62{,}208$ are reachable by any traversal.
  The obstruction is a \emph{cycle} in the constraints, not merely two
  cells sharing two candidate parents: the 16-cell has no such pair and
  still leaves three quarters of its trees unreachable.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: which rules were compared}
  \centering
  {\small
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    & queue & max-index & min-index\\
    \midrule
    Runcinated 24-cell      & $0/240$     & $0/240$     & $0/240$\\
    Runcitruncated 600-cell & $47/2640$   & $2635/2640$ & $2627/2640$\\
    Runcitruncated 120-cell & $2570/2640$ & $2640/2640$ & $2151/2640$\\
    \midrule
    Grand antiprism         & $320/320$   & $320/320$   & \alert{$0/320$}\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \raggedright\medskip
  ``queue'' is the textbook BFS loop, neighbours scanned in index
  order. ``min/max-index'' choose, for each cell, the smallest or
  largest indexed candidate parent.
  \smallskip

  Uniformly random trees are harsher still: of the 61 uniform
  polytopes valid from every root under the queue rule, 13 fail within
  10 random trees --- including all four with $2{,}640$ cells.
\end{frame}
